when will it come back?

You stood at Mammoth Hot Springs and the terraces were white. Dry. Still. The water had stopped. The color was gone. The bacteria that made orange and green and gold  they were gone.

It looked dead.

That was the question in your heart when you saw it.

Because you'd already seen Yellowstone heal once before.

the summer of fire

1.2 million acres. 793,000 inside the park. The sky turned red. The sun turned brown. They said Yellowstone was destroyed.

summer 1988
50 wildfires. Apocalyptic. Blood-red sun through choking brown skies. A century of suppressed fire released all at once.
october 1988
The full extent revealed. Burn scars visible from space. Dark red patches covering western Yellowstone.
1989
One year later. Already: wildflowers in rejuvenated meadows. Fledgling forest replacing burned stands.
Less than 1% of soil was sterilized. The seeds were underground, waiting.
late 1990s  early 2000s
Grasses flourishing. Saplings emerging. The park put up interpretive panels. Three million visitors a year could see the healing firsthand.
You were one of them.
2008
Twenty years later. New pines had taken root. The forest had started to return. Lighter green  young trees growing faster than old ones.
2016
Landsat shows tree cover and grasslands largely recovered. The 28-year-old burn scar had faded beneath green canopy.

you saw it heal in real time

You were young. You went to Yellowstone after the fire. The burn scars were still visible. The standing dead trees. The new growth pushing through ash.

And you watched life come back.

Wildflowers where there had been nothing. Green where there had been black. A whole ecosystem proving that fire isn't death  it's the reset that makes regrowth possible.

Lodgepole pines need fire. Their cones are serotinous  sealed shut by resin, opened only by heat. The fire that "destroyed" the forest was the only thing that could release the next one.

So when you went back, years later, and saw Mammoth Hot Springs looking dead...

It hit different. Because you'd seen healing before. And this didn't look like healing. It looked like loss.

what you saw vs. what it was

This is what alive looks like  and what dormant looks like:

active  water flowing, thermophiles alive

¦ orange = Chloroflexus + cyanobacteria (summer) ¦ green = same bacteria (winter) ¦ cream = filamentous bacteria ¦ blue = water surface

dormant  water redirected underground, calcium carbonate exposed

The white isn't death. It's calcium carbonate  limestone. 500 million years old. It was always there underneath. The color was the life on top. When the water stops flowing on the surface, the thermophiles die, and you see the bone.

But 90% of the water is underground. Always. It didn't leave. It just found a different crack.

why it goes white

The terraces build travertine  1 ton per day. They get heavy. The terrace collapses under its own weight. The water finds a new crack.
Professor Bruce Fouke calls it "the hot springs midlife crisis"  they get overweight and collapse. Then start over somewhere new.
The old theory  that minerals clog the vents  is wrong. The water is slightly acidic underground and keeps minerals dissolved. The vents never clog. The terraces just outgrow themselves.
The color is trillions of thermophilic organisms grouped together. Heat-loving. Orange in summer (carotenoid sunscreen). Green in winter (absorbing more light). They're similar to the first life forms capable of photosynthesis.
The Shoshone and Bannock people collected minerals from these terraces for white paint for hundreds of years. They knew the white. They used it. It was always part of the cycle.

when will the color come back?

3 to 7 years. Not hundreds. Not never. Years.

The water finds a new fracture. It works its way back to the surface. When it arrives, the thermophiles return  within days. The color follows the water like autumn follows summer.

It's not dying. It's shifting. The terraces are living sculptures. They grow, collapse, redirect, regrow. The water is always there  90% underground, 10% visible. What you see is a snapshot of a system that's been doing this for 63,000 years.

The travertine accumulates one million to one billion times faster than in caves, lakes, or oceans. When the water comes back, it comes back fast.

the other cathedral  from the water

Pictured Rocks, Lake Superior. Sandstone cliffs 50 to 200 feet above the water. The colors come from mineral seepage  not bacteria. Geological time, not biological time.

Pictured Rocks in fall  from the water

¦ red/orange = iron ¦ green = copper ¦ blue = copper ¦ black = manganese ¦ white = limonite

Best seen from the water. The boat cruises run from Munising, mid-May through mid-October. Fall color peaks the last week of September. The autumn forest  red, orange, gold  backed by Lake Superior's deep blue. The cliff face painted by millions of years of mineral seepage. No two visits the same.

At Mammoth, the color is alive  bacteria, days old. At Pictured Rocks, the color is geological  minerals, millions of years old. Both are the earth painting itself. One fast. One slow. Both always.

when to go
MOST COLOR ? late June through September
Orange mats brightest in summer  carotenoid sunscreen protects the cyanobacteria from intense sun. More light = more pigment = more orange, more gold.
DEEPEST GREEN ? December through February
Same bacteria, less light. They drop the carotenoid sunscreen and go pure chlorophyll. Dark green mats. Muted but deep. Steam rising through snow. Mammoth is the only entrance open year-round by road.
THE SHIFT ? September / October
The bacteria change color as days shorten  just like leaves. Same mechanism as autumn. You can watch the orange fade to green in real time. Elk rut at Mammoth. Fewer crowds. 70°F days, 40°F nights.
PICTURED ROCKS ? last week of September
Peak fall color at Pictured Rocks. Book the boat from Munising. The cliffs + autumn forest + Lake Superior blue. Earlier is better  heavy rain or wind can knock the leaves down.
