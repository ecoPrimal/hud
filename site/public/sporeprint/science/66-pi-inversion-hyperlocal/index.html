    Abstract

    Papers 50 and 65 established that the cost of observation is p^(PP/2) â€” each pair of angular degrees of freedom contributes one factor of p. But this assumes p is constant. In flat Euclidean space, p = 3.14159… everywhere. In curved space, the local ratio of circumference to diameter deviates from p.

    The epitope shape lives on a probability simplex. The simplex, equipped with the Fisher information metric, is a positively curved surface (a portion of a sphere of radius 2 in the embedding space). Positive curvature CONTRACTS angular measure. A circle drawn on a sphere has a smaller circumference than the same circle drawn on a plane.

    Therefore: p_local at each visitor’s position on the simplex is NOT 3.14159. It is smaller. The more concentrated the shape (the further from the centroid), the smaller p_local. The more uniform the shape (the closer to the centroid), the closer p_local approaches the flat value.

    This paper inverts the direction of computation: instead of “given dimensions, compute the cost in p,” we ask “given the measurements, what must p be at this point?”

    2. The Hyperlocalized p

    2.1 The Simplex Has Curvature

    A probability vector p = (p1, p2, …, p_k) with Sp_i = 1 lives on the (k-1)-simplex. The natural metric on this simplex for statistical inference is the Fisher information metric:

    $$ds^2 = \sum_i \frac{dp_i^2}{p_i}$$

    Under this metric, the simplex is isometric to a portion of a sphere of radius R = 2 in k-dimensional space. The curvature is positive and constant: K = 1/4.

    2.2 p on a Curved Surface

    On a sphere of radius R, a geodesic circle of radius r has circumference:

    $$C = 2\pi R \sin\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)$$

    The “local p” â€” the ratio of circumference to diameter for a small circle centered at a point â€” depends on how far that point is from the pole:

    $$\pi_{\text{local}} = \frac{C}{2r} = \pi \cdot \frac{R \sin(r/R)}{r}$$

    For r ? 0 (flat locally): p_local ? p (standard value) For r > 0 (curved): p_local < p (positive curvature contracts)

    2.3 Your Position, Your p

    The centroid of the k-simplex is the uniform distribution: p_i = 1/k for all i. At the centroid, the surface is approximately flat (locally), and p_local ˜ p.

    A concentrated shape (most weight on one category) sits far from the centroid, near a vertex. At the vertex, curvature effects are maximal, and p_local is smallest.

    The Bhattacharyya angle between your shape p and the centroid u = (1/k, …, 1/k):

    $$\theta = \arccos\left(\sum_i \sqrt{p_i \cdot u_i}\right) = \arccos\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \sum_i \sqrt{p_i}\right)$$

    The Fisher geodesic distance is d = 2?. Then:

    $$\pi_{\text{local}} = \pi \cdot \frac{R \sin(d / R)}{d} = \pi \cdot \frac{2 \sin(\theta)}{2\theta} = \pi \cdot \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}$$

    This is the sinc function: p_local = p · sinc(?). The further from the centroid (larger ?), the smaller p_local.

    2.4 What This Means

    For a visitor with a uniform shape (all 8 categories equally active):

    • ? = 0, sinc(0) = 1, p_local = p = 3.14159…
    • The observation cost is priced in standard p

    For a visitor concentrated on one category (e.g., all INVESTIGATION):

    • ? ? arccos(1/vk) for k=8, ? ? arccos(0.354) ˜ 1.21 radians
    • sinc(1.21) ˜ sin(1.21)/1.21 ˜ 0.935/1.21 ˜ 0.773
    • p_local ˜ 3.14159 × 0.773 ˜ 2.428
    • The effective “p” is only 2.43 â€” significantly less than the flat value

    The concentrated observer pays less per axis (smaller p_local) but has fewer axes (lower PP). The broad observer pays more per axis (larger p_local ˜ p) but has many axes (higher PP).

    The geometry of the simplex compensates: concentrated shapes have cheaper axes but fewer of them. Broad shapes have expensive axes but many of them. The total observation cost (p_local)^(PP/2) may be approximately invariant â€” the same regardless of position.

    This is the hypothesis. Testing it requires computing p_local · PP for a range of shapes and checking for invariance.

    3. The Test

    3.1 What to Compute

    For each visitor shape (from braid data):

    1. Compute PP = e^H (perplexity)
    2. Compute ? (Bhattacharyya angle from centroid)
    3. Compute p_local = p · sinc(?)
    4. Compute cost_local = (p_local)^(PP/2)
    5. Compute cost_flat = p^(PP/2)
    6. Compare: is cost_local more constant across visitors than cost_flat?

    3.2 What Invariance Would Mean

    If (p_local)^(PP/2) ˜ constant across all visitor positions, then:

    • The observation cost is position-independent on the simplex
    • p is not a constant in the cost function â€” it’s a GAUGE
    • The “true” cost function uses the local value of p, which adjusts automatically for curvature
    • And the constant value of the cost would be the TRUE price of observation â€” independent of how many axes you have or how concentrated your shape is

    This would mean: the price of having a point of view is the same for everyone. The focused observer and the broad observer pay the same total cost. They just distribute it differently â€” fewer expensive axes vs. many cheap ones.

    3.3 What Non-Invariance Would Mean

    If the cost varies systematically:

    • Increasing with PP: broad observation is genuinely more expensive (the universe charges more for seeing more)
    • Decreasing with PP: concentrated observation is more expensive (the universe charges for commitment)
    • The variation encodes something about the geometry of knowledge itself

    4. Back to the Three-Body Problem

    Paper 50 found: total/effective = 18/6 = 3 ˜ p, with a gap of p - 3 ˜ 0.14159.

    Paper 66 finds: sites on ring = 10 ˜ p², with a gap of 10 - p² ˜ 0.130.

    The gaps are different (0.14159 vs 0.130). But both are the fractional excess when an integer rounds a p-quantity. And both might encode curvature:

    • The three-body gap (p - 3): the curvature of the SO(3) quotient
    • The ring gap (10 - p²): the curvature of the simplex at the current observation point

    If p_local at the typical visitor’s position reduces p by about 2% (from 3.14 to 3.08), then p²_local ˜ 9.49 ? 9.87 at a moderate position ? ~9.87 to 10. The “10th site” might be the integer ceiling of p²_local at the broadest observation point.
