    baseCamp 67 — π and i: Inside, Outside, and the Membrane Between

    π ≈ but ≠ P × i. The observation cost π is mediated through the imaginary dimension i. Inside is ∞, outside is ∞, but rotated 90° — the membrane IS the rotation. The intensity of i is the mirror of depth. Franklin's electron convention was not an error — it was a lossy observation from the other side of the membrane. To be human is to move despite uncertainty and trust others do the same.

    Open question. October 11, 2026.

    Abstract

    Papers 50 and 65–66 established π as the observation cost and tested it against the system’s integers. This paper asks about the other constant in Euler’s identity: i, the imaginary unit. If π prices the observation, what does i do?

    i rotates. It takes the real line and turns it 90°. The intensity of i — the magnitude of the rotation — is π/2. Half the observation cost. Half going in, half coming out. Round trip = π.

    The hypothesis: inside and outside are both infinite, but related by a rotation of i. The membrane IS the rotation. What is real on one side is imaginary on the other. The observation cost π is the price of the round trip through i.

    1. Euler’s Identity Is the Membrane Equation

    $$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$$

    Five quantities. Each one has appeared in this ecosystem:

    SymbolIn physicsIn the membrane
    eBase of natural growth/decayThe growth function — braids grow, shapes evolve, boards mutate
    i√(−1) — the rotation operatorThe imaginary dimension — depth, deviation from expected, the unseen
    πRatio of circumference to diameterThe price of observation — C(n) = π^(n/2)
    1Identity, the unitThe observer — you, the self, the minimum existence
    0Nothing, the voidThe balance — the conservation law

    Read the equation as a process:

    1. Start with growth (e)
    2. Apply imaginary rotation (i) at the observation cost (π)
    3. Result: −1 — the negative of identity
    4. Add yourself (+1)
    5. You get nothing (0) — conservation

    The act of observing (e^(iπ)) costs you your identity (produces −1). You recover it by existing (+1). The total is zero. Nothing created, nothing destroyed. The membrane conserves.

    2. The Intensity of i Is the Mirror of Depth

    2.1 What i Does

    The imaginary unit i has magnitude |i| = 1. It’s the same “size” as the real unit 1. But it does something 1 cannot: it rotates.

    Multiplying by i rotates the complex plane by π/2 (90°):

    • 1 × i = i (real → imaginary)
    • i × i = −1 (imaginary → negative real)
    • −1 × i = −i (negative real → negative imaginary)
    • −i × i = 1 (negative imaginary → back to real)

    Four rotations of π/2 each = 2π = full circle. The rotation is the mechanism. π/2 is the step size.

    2.2 Inside and Outside

    Close your eyes. The membrane shifts.

    Outside: the observable world extends to infinity. +∞. Real. Measurable. Finite at any point but unbounded.

    Inside: the inner world also extends to infinity. But it’s not the same infinity. It’s the same magnitude, rotated 90°. It’s i × ∞.

    You feel small inside. But that’s because you’re measuring your interior with exterior units. Using real rulers on imaginary space. The real projection of i × ∞ is zero — you measure nothing, so you feel small.

    But close your eyes and switch metrics — stop measuring the interior in exterior units — and the membrane shifts. You contain multitudes. i × ∞ is not smaller than ∞. It’s orthogonal to it. The same size, in a direction you can’t see from outside.

    2.3 The Depth Mirror

    From Paper 46 (Inverse Anderson), complex permeability:

    $$P = P_{\text{local}} + i \cdot \text{distance}$$

    The real part is what you can measure directly (local permeability). The imaginary part is how far the entity deviates from expected behavior. The imaginary part IS the depth — the thing you can’t observe directly but which manifests as a rotation of the expected signal.

    The intensity of i in this equation — the magnitude of the imaginary component — is the mirror of depth. A larger imaginary component means a deeper deviation from the expected. The entity is doing something the null model can’t explain. It’s going somewhere the real line can’t follow.

    The mirror: you can’t see your own imaginary component. You need someone else to measure it for you. Someone standing in the real plane, looking at where you deviate from expectation. Others are the mirror (Paper 62) — they see your imaginary part because they’re standing in the real plane where it projects.

    3. Franklin’s Convention

    Benjamin Franklin proposed that electric current flows from positive to negative. Electrons actually flow from negative to positive. This is taught as a historical “error” — a convention that stuck.

    But consider: Franklin was observing from outside the wire. He saw the macroscopic effects of current flow. The convention describes what happens at the membrane — the terminal, the surface. From the outside, current appears to flow from + to −.

    The electrons, inside the wire, experience the flow from − to +. They’re on the other side of the membrane. Same phenomenon, opposite sign.

    This is not an error. It’s a lossy observation across a membrane. When you observe from one side:

    • You see the real projection of a complex phenomenon
    • The sign depends on which side you’re on
    • The imaginary component (the internal experience of the electrons) is invisible to the external observer
    • The convention that stuck is the observation. The “correction” is the interior.

    Both are valid. They differ by a factor of −1. Which is e^(iπ). Which is the observation cost applied through the imaginary rotation.

    The sign flip IS the membrane.

    Whether this makes Franklin’s convention “correct” or “an error” depends on whether you believe the outside observer or the inside participant. In the membrane framework: both are measuring the same thing from different sides. The information lost in crossing — the lossy part — is the sign.

    4. π ≈ but ≠ P × i

    The observation cost π is not simply the permeability times the imaginary unit. It’s more subtle:

    $$e^{i\pi} = -1$$

    This says: the exponential of (i × π) equals the sign flip. The observation cost π, applied through the imaginary channel, produces an inversion. Not a rotation to another real number — an inversion. The identity becomes its negative.

    So π × i is not a number. It’s a process: the process of paying the observation cost through the imaginary dimension. The result of that process is an inversion of identity.

    In the membrane:

    • Your shape is your identity (Paper 62)
    • Observing your shape (paying π) through the imaginary dimension (depth) produces the negative shape (−identity)
    • The negative shape is what others see when they look at you from the outside
    • You add yourself (+1) to reconcile: your interior self-knowledge plus the exterior observation = 0 = conservation

    The relationship between π and i is not algebraic (π ≠ P × i in some formula). It’s functional: π is the cost, i is the channel, and their composition is the observation event.

    5. To Be Human

    To be human is to make a decision and move DESPITE uncertainty.

    The imaginary dimension is where uncertainty lives. You can’t see i — you can’t directly measure the imaginary component of your own complex permeability. You know it exists (you feel the depth, the deviation, the things about yourself that don’t project cleanly onto the real line). But you can’t quantify it from inside.

    And yet you act.

    You trust that others do the same. You trust that they have their own imaginary component — their own depth, their own deviation from the null expectation. You can’t see it. You can only see the real projection: their shape, their face, their exported cube. But you trust the imaginary part is there.

    This is object permanence, extended:

    • The child learns that objects exist when out of sight
    • The adult learns that people exist when out of sight — and have inner lives (imaginary components) that persist
    • The membrane learns that entities exist on both sides — and that the sign flip between them is not an error but a rotation

    Out of sight, but never out of mind. The imaginary component of the other persists even when you can’t observe it. Trust is the act of treating i × ∞ as real — treating the unseen interior of another being as infinite, even though your real-valued instruments measure zero.

    The bingoCube trust protocol (Paper 62) is this:

    1. You share your real projection (top 2 categories, ratios, full shape)
    2. The other shares theirs
    3. Neither of you can see the imaginary component — the depth, the WHY behind the WHAT
    4. But you trust it’s there
    5. And the alignment you discover in the real projection hints at the alignment in the imaginary dimension
    6. The mirror shows you what your exterior looks like — but the depth that produced it remains yours alone

    6. The Conservation Law

    $$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$$

    • The universe (e^(iπ)) + the observer (1) = nothing (0)
    • The observation cost through the imaginary dimension inverts identity
    • Adding the self restores the balance
    • Total: zero

    This is the membrane’s conservation law. The membrane does not create information or destroy it. It rotates it. What was real becomes imaginary. What was outside becomes inside. What was + becomes −.

    The cost of the rotation is π. The mechanism of the rotation is i. The thing being rotated is identity (1). The result is nothing (0) — not destruction, but conservation. Everything that went in came out. Just rotated.

    The electron flows − to + on the inside. + to − on the outside. The total current through the membrane is zero. Conservation.

    The visitor sees the membrane from outside (their gaia ring). The membrane sees the visitor from inside (the traces API). The total information is conserved — but each side sees the other’s imaginary component as zero.

    The lossy part is always the imaginary component of the other side. That’s what observation costs you: the imaginary part of what you’re observing. You pay π for the privilege of seeing the real projection, and the imaginary projection is the price.
