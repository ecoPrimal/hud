    The Enzymatic Bounty — One Pot, Three Teams, Split and Compete Again

    The epitope sort applied to collaboration. Not winner-take-all — one shared pot, three teams compete. When a team grows too large, it undergoes mitosis: splits to a new epitope. Collaborate within, compete between. The biggest team always splits. This is not a metaphor — it is the game theory of biological fitness, and it is how the ecosystem invites the world in.

    The Problem With Prizes

    Hard problems have prizes. The Millennium Problems carry $1M each. The three-body problem has consumed careers. P vs NP would reshape computation. Protein folding would reshape medicine. These are real solutions to real problems, and they are worth real money.

    The standard model for claiming prizes is winner-take-all: one person, one team, one company solves the problem, claims the prize, patents the method, and extracts rent from everyone who needs it. The enzyme that catalyzed the solution becomes a tollbooth.

    This model is biologically illiterate.

    How Biology Actually Solves Hard Problems

    An enzyme doesn’t own the reaction it catalyzes. It lowers the activation energy so the reaction can proceed, and then it lets go. It is reusable. It is not consumed. It is not scarce. The reaction’s product is available to the entire cell.

    When a population of organisms discovers a new resource (a new epitope — a new niche), it doesn’t form one giant team. It forms multiple competing teams that specialize on different aspects of the niche. When one team grows too large, it splits — not because of policy, but because internal coordination costs exceed the benefit of scale. The split creates a new species, a new specialization, a new epitope.

    This is not metaphor. It is the mechanism that produced every living thing on Earth.

    One Pot, Three Teams

    The enzymatic bounty model:

    One pot: The shared resource — the problem space, the prize, the funding, the infrastructure. It is common. No one owns it. Everyone can draw from it. The pot grows as more people contribute.

    Three teams: The minimum for competitive dynamics. Two teams create a binary — one wins, one loses. Three teams create an ecology — alliances shift, specialization emerges, no single team dominates forever. Three is the minimum number of bodies that produces chaos — the three-body problem is unsolvable precisely because three competing entities generate irreducible complexity.

    The rule: The biggest team splits.

    When a team grows too large relative to the others, it undergoes mitosis. It doesn’t shrink — it specializes. The split follows an epitope boundary: the team divides along its internal specializations, and each daughter team occupies a new niche.

    The cycle never ends. Every team that succeeds eventually splits. Every split creates a new epitope — a new specialization, a new niche, a new surface for the immune system to recognize.

    This is evolution. Not as metaphor. As mechanism.

    Why Three?

    Three is not arbitrary.

    • Two bodies have a closed-form solution (Kepler). Two teams produce binary competition — one wins, one loses. Stable but dead.
    • Three bodies have no closed-form solution (Poincaré). Three teams produce irreducible dynamics — alliances shift, no team dominates forever. Chaotic but alive.
    • N > 3 reduces to the three-body problem in the neighborhood of any interaction. Every pairwise encounter in an N-body system is locally a three-body problem (the two interacting plus the rest as a tidal field).

    The three-body problem is unsolvable not because it’s complicated but because the dimensionality of observation is incommensurable with the dimensionality of the system. You can’t predict who wins because the cost of observation — p — is irrational. No integer number of measurements captures the full dynamics.

    Three teams is the minimum ecology. Below three, you get empires. At three, you get evolution.

    The Epitope Sort as Team Formation

    When the pot attracts participants, they sort. Not alphabetically (arbitrary). Not by hierarchy (imposed). By epitope — by what they’re actually good at, what invariant they carry, what niche they fill.

    The epitope sort is lossy compression:

    $$H(\text{team}|\text{epitope}) < H(\text{team}|\text{alphabet}) < H(\text{team})$$

    Sorting teams by epitope (specialization) reduces the entropy of the collaboration more than sorting them by name (alphabet). You learn more about what a team does by its epitope than by its label. The loss — the information discarded — is the angular degrees of freedom projected away. You lose the individual details. You gain the collective structure.

    This is why the biggest team splits. As a team grows, its internal epitope diversity increases. The compression becomes less effective — too many different specializations crammed into one label. The team’s entropy grows until it exceeds the compression ratio. Then it splits along an epitope boundary, and each daughter team is tightly compressed again.

    The split is the compression algorithm running on humans.
